\section*{Executive synthesis }
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Executive synthesis }

In composed cloud-edge services, individual providers may advertise limited workload and SLA evidence while keeping their stochastic execution models private. The methodological challenge is to combine these separate disclosures into a prediction of end-to-end SLA satisfaction over a time horizon, without assuming access to hidden process parameters. We report the development, implementation, and evaluation of a family of methods indexed by provider information representation $I$ and integration procedure $M$, together with a simulator and an account of observed limitations. This internal report records the experiments and their evidential limits. It does not propose a general validation methodology for management or control algorithms.

We define the evaluated quantity as $\sigma_G(A,H;\rho)=\Pr[c_G(A,H)\ge\rho]$, where $c_G(A,H)$ is the fraction of \textbf{decided} composed requests that meet a specified latency-cost-quality region by time $H$. A missed latency deadline is a decided failure; requests not yet decided are excluded; zero decided requests yield compliance one. This quantity measures the probability that the composed service meets its SLA compliance target over the observation horizon $H$.

\textbf{First, the controlled pilot (\hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase I})} tested the original three-provider parallel graph. The original controlled pilot was promising. Against a fresh white-box reference ($N_{\mathrm{WB}}=200$: 200 new simulation runs using the true underlying model), across 555 related measurements (15 SLA conditions, each evaluated at 37 time horizons using shared simulation runs), the mean absolute errors (MAE) of a single reconstructed model (M1), a 27-member equal-weight ensemble (M2), and a Gibbs-weighted pilot M3 with 14 propagated joint models were respectively \textbf{0.18517, 0.06815 and 0.03037}. However, a retrospective cost-matched support audit found a Top1 estimator with MAE \textbf{0.02878}, slightly below the weighted Top14 estimator in that fixed pilot. The pilot therefore did not establish that multi-model averaging was necessary.

\textbf{Next, the expanded evaluation (\hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase II})} evaluated generalization across multiple provider configurations and composition graphs. Sixteen evaluation cases were planned (four provider configurations combined with four composition graphs). The four cases involving configuration P2 did not meet the predefined criteria for informative SLA test conditions, so they were excluded without replacement. On the remaining 12 cells, with independent $N_{\mathrm{WB}}=1000$ final white-box trajectories per cell and 6,660 related measurements (555 per case) per fully applicable method, the executed \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-5 V3b} algorithms produced MAE \textbf{0.15702} (M1), \textbf{0.13395} (M2), and \textbf{0.12189} (M3 FULL343). M3 retained the best aggregate ranking, but hard conditions still had substantial errors. Its algorithm and candidate support also changed from the pilot and from the initially registered \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-5} method. Accordingly these numbers cannot be treated as direct replications of the original 14-member method.

\textbf{Finally, the retrospective investigation (\hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase III})} investigates that gap, including an original-style M3 comparison, without creating a new independent holdout. Reconstructing 48 public-I1-scored candidates/provider and propagating Top14 at B=1400 on the same 12 cells gave \textbf{MAE 0.15416} (separately simulated Top1, \textbf{0.16228}), versus \textbf{0.12189} for executed V3b FULL343. Although Top14 retained at least \textbf{99.64\%} of inferred joint-model weight in P1, P3 and P4, FULL343 outperformed it in \textbf{11 of 12} cells. These post-result comparisons do not isolate member count or constitute a fresh holdout.

Subsequent development tests revealed further limitations. On P4/G\_SEQPAR, stronger reconstruction from I1 alone performed comparably to, and on average slightly better than, several richer I2 information-and-loss pipelines under matched search and graph-propagation designs. This does \textbf{not} isolate the causal utility of information quantity because fitting objectives also changed. In a separate P2/G\_PAR experiment using a deterministically composed public graph region, M1, M2 and M3 achieved MAE \textbf{0.13200, 0.14058 and 0.12787}, respectively, under \textbf{unmatched} graph budgets. A privileged retrospective audit then found that the true generating provider parameters fitted the public I1 evidence better than the selected reconstructed candidates despite lying inside the search domain. This is direct evidence of a search/reconstruction deficit, not a proof of structural non-identifiability.

The overall conclusion is conditional: end-to-end SLA estimation from limited disclosure is demonstrably feasible \textbf{in some controlled cases}, but the developed finite-search integration methods have not delivered reliable prediction across the studied variations. This report preserves favorable, negative, and inconclusive findings, including differences between the single-graph pilot and the broader battery, incomplete Step-0 eligibility, unequal simulation budgets, and limits of the reported uncertainty measures. These observations provide \textbf{initial empirical motivation}, not a validated procedure, for a separate methodological paper on evaluating management, control, and optimization algorithms in hierarchical computing-continuum systems. That future work is intended to address how to select evaluation graphs and scenarios, choose simulation sample sizes, and estimate errors and uncertainties before making broader performance claims.

\section*{1. Research problem, scope, and prior context}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{1. Research problem, scope, and prior context}

\subsection*{1.1 Composable offerings and information boundaries}

The computing continuum spans resources and services controlled by different administrative entities. An integrator may know its own workflow or service-composition graph while observing only restricted public claims about the providers on which it depends. Physical execution is shaped by stochastic service times, queues, branch synchronization, workload and cost accumulation. Predicting the performance of composed black-box services using only their providers' advertised performance measures is not equivalent to observing the resulting end-to-end behavior.

More generally, empirical claims about management and control algorithms for hierarchical computing-continuum systems depend on the selected graph structures, workload and provider conditions, simulation budgets, and reference measurements. This report studies one specific prediction problem and documents what its experiments establish. The broader question of how to design such evaluations systematically is reserved for future methodological work.

The Composable Autonomic Offering (CAO) abstraction represents an offering as

\begin{equation*}
o=(G,s,\sigma,r), \tag{1}
\end{equation*}

where $G$ is the composition graph, $s$ describes service objectives, $\sigma$ represents dependability or survival information, and $r$ is the realization envelope [12]. The CAO abstraction motivates predicting a useful dependability quantity, but does not, by itself, prescribe which information must be disclosed or which numerical method can produce a dependable composition estimate.

We separate these choices explicitly:

\begin{equation*}
\tau=(I,M), \tag{2}
\end{equation*}

with \textbf{information interface} $I$ and \textbf{integration method} $M$. The interface $I$ specifies what each provider discloses. Given these separate disclosures and the known composition graph $G$, the integrator applies $M$ to estimate the composed service's end-to-end SLA probability, $\widehat\sigma_G(A,H;\rho)$. Depending on the method, this may involve reconstructing provider models and propagating their predictions through $G$. Distinct $M$ may use the same $I$; conversely an information-rich interface can require a different reconstruction objective, making the evaluated unit an \textbf{information–method pair}.

The question is not whether analytical or stochastic QoS composition exists. It does [3,8,17–19]. Rather, the specific problem is \textbf{inverse, public-evidence-constrained} composition: can an integrator combine the probability surfaces advertised by individual black-box providers to estimate the composed service's finite-horizon, end-to-end SLA probability, without access to their execution traces or true parameters?

\subsection*{1.2 Research questions and evidence categories}

The program addresses four related questions:

\textbf{RQ1, feasibility and limits.} When can finite public provider disclosures support useful graph-level SLA-satisfaction prediction, and in which worlds, topologies, regimes, or query regions does prediction fail?

\textbf{RQ2, methods under a fixed interface.} How do analytical propagation, a single provider reconstruction, equal-weight reconstruction portfolios and evidence-weighted finite supports compare? Which observed differences are due to candidate search, support membership, weights or numerical allocation, and which cannot be causally separated?

\textbf{RQ3, disclosure.} Does enriching the public interface with marginal or paired temporal compliance information improve the resulting \emph{information–loss–integration pipeline} under matched reconstruction resources?

\textbf{RQ4, provenance and decision meaning.} How reproducible are the finite results, and what do numerical Monte Carlo intervals, model spread, reference error and support-truncation guarantees actually mean for an SLA threshold?

We classify the evidence by its scientific role, rather than by the names of repository folders or successive software versions. \textbf{First, a controlled pilot (\hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase I})} tested the methods in a single provider world and graph; it established a promising initial result, not cross-setting reliability. \textbf{Next, the expanded evaluation (\hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase II})} tested multiple worlds and composition graphs using a final white-box reference held back until predictions were frozen. Its implementation was revised during development, so this is not an unchanged confirmation of the pilot method. \textbf{Finally, retrospective diagnosis and further development (\hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase III})} compared historical variants, investigated divergence and explored richer provider disclosures after earlier results were known. These later tests do not constitute another independent holdout. Appendix C provides the experiment-by-experiment chronology and exact method-version identifiers.

\subsection*{1.3 Scientific boundaries}

This document does not establish a structural identifiability theorem. Multiple candidate models with different parameters and similar \emph{finite empirical} I1 errors may reflect a non-injective information map, measurement noise, search limitations, model family restrictions, or some combination. The present observations cannot distinguish these causes in full generality.

The report is an \textbf{empirical record}, not a proposed or validated general-purpose experimental protocol. Its findings apply to the particular provider worlds, graph structures, query-selection rules, budgets and reference procedures actually tested. They motivate further methodological work but do not determine which graphs, sample sizes or uncertainty estimators should be used to validate other hierarchical management and control algorithms.

\section*{2. Formal SLA objective and benchmark semantics}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{2. Formal SLA objective and benchmark semantics}

\subsection*{2.1 Request-level admissibility}

For a composed request, denote latency, accumulated cost and quality by $(L_G,C_G,Q_G)$. A rectangular admissibility region is

\begin{equation*}
A=\{(L,C,Q): L\le\ell,\ C\le c,\ Q\ge q\}.
\end{equation*}

A request is \textbf{compliant} when it is decided and its final outcome lies in $A$. The simulator records the top-level emission and, if available before simulation stop, the completion time. An on-time request is decided at completion. An uncompleted or late request is decided as a latency failure when its deadline arrives. A request whose completion and deadline are still in the future is unresolved at the current evaluation horizon. The SLA-scoring rule does not equate “unresolved” with “failed”.

Let $\mathcal D_G(H)$ be the set of top-level requests \emph{decided} by horizon $H$, and $\mathcal C_G(A,H)\subseteq\mathcal D_G(H)$ the compliant subset. Define, with the explicitly frozen zero-decision convention,

\begin{equation*}
c_G(A,H)=
\begin{cases}
1, &|\mathcal D_G(H)|=0,\\[2mm]
|\mathcal C_G(A,H)|/|\mathcal D_G(H)|, &|\mathcal D_G(H)|>0.
\end{cases}
\tag{3}
\end{equation*}

For required compliance fraction $\rho\in[0,1]$, the true graph-level estimand is

\begin{equation*}
\boxed{\sigma_G(A,H;\rho)=\Pr\{c_G(A,H)\ge\rho\}.}
\tag{4}
\end{equation*}

The probability is taken over independent stochastic trajectories of the specified provider world, workload and execution graph. The horizon $H$ determines which decided requests are included in the compliance fraction. As more requests are decided, this fraction may rise or fall, so $\sigma_G(A,H;\rho)$ is \textbf{not guaranteed to decrease with $H$}. We therefore use the precise term “horizon-dependent SLA-satisfaction probability” throughout the report.

The numerical methods compute $\widehat\sigma_G^{(I,M)}(A,H;\rho)$, and the hidden world is only evaluated through a separate finite white-box reference $\widehat\sigma_G^{\mathrm{WB}}$ under experimental conditions that permit it.

\subsection*{2.2 Prediction target and evaluation reference}
\phantomsection\label{sec:prediction-target}

\textbf{What do we estimate?} An SLA prediction query $(A,H;\rho)$ specifies an admissibility region $A$ (latency, cost and quality limits), an observation horizon $H$, and a required fraction $\rho$ of decided requests that must comply with $A$ by $H$. For a known composition graph $G$, Equation (4) defines $\sigma_G(A,H;\rho)$, the probability that the composed service meets this requirement. The integrator does not know the providers' hidden stochastic execution models. It has only the information each provider discloses through interface $I$. Integration method $M$ uses those disclosures and the known graph to produce the end-to-end prediction $\widehat\sigma_G^{(I,M)}(A,H;\rho)$. \textbf{The scientific task is to assess the accuracy and limits of this probability prediction.}

\textbf{How do we evaluate it?} In the simulator, the true provider models can be used to generate an independent finite white-box reference $\widehat\sigma_G^{\mathrm{WB}}(A,H;\rho)$. We compare each method's prediction with this reference across the tested SLA queries, horizons, provider worlds and graph topologies. The hidden provider models used for final reference generation are not supplied to the methods being evaluated. Because the reference is computed from a finite number of stochastic runs, it is itself an estimate of $\sigma_G$, not an exact value. Numerical simulation error and reconstruction error must therefore be distinguished (Section 9 and Appendix A).

\section*{3. Provider disclosures}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{3. Provider disclosures}

\subsection*{3.1 Public interface I1}

Under the original public interface I1, each provider advertises a finite family of rectangular admissibility regions indexed by a target content level $\rho_r$:

\begin{equation*}
A_i(\rho_r)=
\{L_i\le\ell_i(\rho_r),\, C_i\le c_i(\rho_r),\, Q_i\ge q_i\}.
\tag{5}
\end{equation*}

Here $L_i$, $C_i$ and $Q_i$ are the provider's latency, cost and quality outcomes. The functions $\ell_i(\rho_r)$ and $c_i(\rho_r)$ specify \textbf{numerical latency and cost limits} for each advertised index $\rho_r$. They are finite lookup mappings, not time-varying performance functions. In this simulated experiment, the framework prepares a separate I1 card on behalf of each provider and makes it available to the integrator. To construct its advertised regions, the provider-side procedure fits a joint model of private latency and cost observations (a Gaussian mixture in log latency and log cost). It draws a fixed synthetic sample from that model and, for each $\rho_r$, chooses a minimum-area rectangle containing at least that fraction of sampled outcomes, while retaining the preceding region. Consequently the regions are \textbf{nested}: when $\rho_r<\rho_r'$, $A_i(\rho_r)\subseteq A_i(\rho_r')$, so neither the latency nor the cost limit decreases. In the studied provider worlds, the quality outcome is constant at $Q_i=0.5$, with advertised limit $q_i=0.5$. Thus quality is not a varied experimental dimension, and the regions differ only in their latency and cost limits.

The index $\rho_r$ thus specifies the \textbf{target content of the fitted latency-cost model}, not a guaranteed fraction of compliant requests during an execution horizon. The latter is the separate SLA-compliance threshold $\rho_q$. Once the regions are fixed, each provider advertises an empirical probability surface

\begin{equation*}
\widehat\sigma_i^{I1}(A_i(\rho_r),H;\rho_q)
\approx \Pr\{c_i(A_i(\rho_r),H)\ge\rho_q\}.
\tag{6}
\end{equation*}

The historical pilot used $\rho_r,\rho_q\in\{0.95,0.975,0.98333,0.99,0.995\}$ and a 5-second horizon grid up to 240 seconds, with a workload period of 0.2 seconds. Provider-card acquisition used two disjoint banks of 100 complete simulation trajectories each. The first bank (seeds 6000--6099) was used only to fit the provider's latency-cost model and construct the regions. The second bank (seeds 6100--6199) evaluated the already fixed regions. For each $(A_i(\rho_r),H,\rho_q)$, each trajectory in the second bank contributed one binary outcome: whether its decided-request compliance fraction reached $\rho_q$ by horizon $H$. Thus $\widehat\sigma_i^{I1}=n_{\mathrm{pass}}/100$ is the fraction of successful trajectories, \textbf{not} an average of raw traces, the mean of per-trajectory compliance fractions, or the fraction of compliant requests pooled across trajectories. The same trajectories are reused across regions, horizons and compliance thresholds, so the points on the probability surface are correlated. Each simulation run also supplies provider-local ledgers for all three providers.

The public I1 card includes pointwise 95\% Wilson binomial confidence intervals for each estimated SLA-satisfaction probability (the chance that a new independent provider trajectory achieves at least the required fraction $\rho_q$ of compliant decided requests for region $A_i(\rho_r)$ by horizon $H$). These intervals are disclosed as part of I1, but none of the evaluated methods M0, M1, M2 or M3 uses them. The card does \textbf{not} disclose request timestamps, hidden queue/service trajectories or provider parameters.

The provider-side procedure uses private simulation evidence to construct the finite I1 card. The integrator, by design, sees only that card. This provider-versus-integrator information boundary is essential: giving a method the hidden sample identities, generating parameters or white-box graph predictions would change $I$ and invalidate the intended inverse reconstruction comparison.

Note that I1 discloses provider-level regions $A_i$, not the graph-level SLA query region $A_G$ for the composed service. For each experimental comparison, $A_G$ is fixed and shared by the methods being evaluated.

\subsection*{3.2 Enriched I2 interfaces}

The post-result I1/I2 experiments were developed in the repository's \path{first_science/phase6/} folder (historical \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-6}) and belong to global \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase III} in this report. They retain the I1 provider regions and horizon grid and reuse the corresponding provider-local simulation evidence. What changes is the information disclosed to the integrator about how often, and how consistently, each provider satisfies its advertised regions.

\textbf{I2a: distribution of compliance across simulation runs.} Fix one advertised provider region and one evaluation horizon. In each independent simulation run, the provider handles many requests. We calculate the percentage of requests decided by that horizon that satisfy the region's latency, cost and quality limits. This gives \textbf{one compliance percentage per run}. To illustrate, suppose four runs produce 98\%, 96\%, 94\% and 70\% compliance. If the SLA requires at least 95\%, two runs satisfy it and two do not. For this threshold, I1 would report only the pass rate, 2 out of 4 runs (50\%). I2a would instead disclose the four compliance percentages themselves, sorted as 70\%, 94\%, 96\% and 98\%. The integrator can therefore see not only how often the SLA is met, but also how much compliance varies and how severely it can fall below the requirement. In the actual experiment, \textbf{100 independent runs}, rather than four, provide 100 compliance percentages for each advertised region and horizon. These percentages are sorted separately for every horizon, so I2a does not reveal which values at different horizons came from the same run.

\textbf{I2b: paired compliance at two horizons.} I2b includes the I2a distributions and adds pairs of compliance fractions measured in the \emph{same} trajectory at two selected horizons. A pair such as (0.98, 0.91) means that cumulative compliance in one run declined from 98\% to 91\% between those horizons; the fractions still refer to all requests decided since the start, not just requests processed within the interval. For each selected interval, the published representation contains 100 such pairs. The frozen design samples intervals starting at 5 seconds and then every 10 seconds, with separations of 20, 30, 40, 50, 75, 100, 150 or 200 seconds where both endpoints fit within the 240-second horizon. The pairs reveal whether good or poor compliance tends to persist or change over time. They contain no trajectory identifiers, and the published records cannot be linked across different intervals by a shared identifier; they are not complete request histories or labelled trajectories.

\textbf{Additional disclosure in I2b-v4.} The later I2b-v4 extension publishes one more value for each selected interval: the average, across the 100 runs, of the difference between the highest and lowest cumulative compliance fractions recorded during that interval on the 5-second evaluation grid. This describes variation \emph{inside} an interval that its two endpoint values alone cannot reveal. Individual runs' ranges and complete histories remain private. Section 4.3 explains how M2 uses I1, I2a, I2b and this additional summary to reconstruct provider models. The results appear in Section 8.3.

\section*{4. Integration algorithms and historical method versions}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{4. Integration algorithms and historical method versions}

\subsection*{4.1 M0: analytical public-boundary predictor}

M0 asks a worst-case question: \textbf{If every provider meets its advertised limits for a request, what limits follow for the complete service?} The advertised region $A_i(\rho_r)$ (Eq. (5)) specifies maximum latency $\ell_i$, maximum cost $c_i$ and minimum quality $q_i$. In a sequence, latencies add. In an all-required parallel join whose branches start together, latency is determined by the slowest branch. Cost adds because every required service incurs cost. The model defines composed quality as the minimum across required services. Before including processing and communication overheads, the limits are

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
\operatorname{Seq}:&\quad
\ell=\sum_i\ell_i,\quad c=\sum_i c_i,\quad q=\min_i q_i,\\
\operatorname{ParAll}:&\quad
\ell=\max_i\ell_i,\quad c=\sum_i c_i,\quad q=\min_i q_i.
\end{aligned}
\tag{7}
\end{equation*}

These limits apply to a request only if all required providers respect their advertised limits. M0 applies the same rules recursively to graphs built from sequence and all-required parallel operations. For example, in $G=A\to(B\parallel C)$, the provider-only latency limit is $\ell_A+\max(\ell_B,\ell_C)$, cost is $c_A+c_B+c_C$, and quality is $\min(q_A,q_B,q_C)$. Fixed processing, network and synchronization costs or delays are included at their actual positions in the graph.

For the frozen \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-I} pilot, Source $\to$ Fpre $\to$ $(A\parallel B\parallel C)$ $\to$ Fpost, three network-data hops contribute $0.001001$ s each, a completion-control message contributes $0.001$ s, and Fpre and Fpost contribute $0.005$ s each. Thus the additional latency is $3(0.001001)+0.001+2(0.005)=0.014003$ s. The two fixed services add $0.015+0.015=0.03$ cost units, while network costs are zero. The resulting limits are $\ell_G^{M0}=0.014003+\max_i\ell_i$, $c_G^{M0}=0.03+\sum_i c_i$ and $q_G^{M0}=\min_i q_i$. These constants come from the simulator configuration and must be recalculated for other graph configurations.

Applying these rules to the advertised I1 regions gives the M0-induced region $A_G^{I1+M0}$, which need not equal the requested graph-level SLA region $A_G$. For example, M0 may calculate an $80$ ms latency limit while the requested SLA allows $100$ ms. M0 makes a prediction only when $A_G^{I1+M0}\subseteq A_G$: its calculated maximum latency and cost do not exceed the requested maxima, and its minimum quality meets the requested minimum. Otherwise it reports \textbf{not applicable}, which does not mean the service will fail. Provided the required I1 values are available, the prediction is

\begin{equation*}
\widehat\sigma_G^{M0}(A_G,H;\rho)
=\prod_{i\in\{A,B,C\}}
\widehat\sigma_i^{I1}(A_i(\rho),H;\rho).
\tag{8}
\end{equation*}

M0 estimates the SLA probability from the providers' I1 cards; the white-box reference evaluates the requested graph-level region $A_G$ directly. With fixed cards, $H$ and $\rho$, M0 gives the same probability for every requested region containing $A_G^{I1+M0}$, even though the white-box probability can change with $A_G$. The estimate can also be wrong when the regions coincide: providers can fail on different requests, and the product in Eq. (8) does not account for those different failure sets or for execution coupling.

\subsection*{4.2 M1: one reconstructed provider process per provider}

M0 estimates the end-to-end SLA-satisfaction probability (Eqs.~(3) and (4)) using analytical composition rules and the probabilities advertised by individual providers. M1 follows a different approach. It reconstructs one stochastic execution model for each provider from its public I1 information, connects these models according to the known composition graph $G$, and estimates the end-to-end SLA probability through simulation. Unlike M0, M1 evaluates the requested graph-level SLA region $A_G$ directly in the simulated executions of the reconstructed system.

Each reconstructed provider is represented by three parameters:

\begin{equation*}
\theta_i=(\mu_i,\kappa_i,\mathrm{CV}_i),
\tag{9}
\end{equation*}

where $\mu_i$ is the mean processing time, $\kappa_i$ is the cost per unit of processing time, and $\mathrm{CV}_i$ is the coefficient of variation of processing time, defined as its standard deviation divided by its mean. Processing times follow a gamma distribution with the specified mean and variability. This parameterization defines the family of candidate provider models used by M1, M2 and M3.

For each provider, M1 searches for parameters whose simulated I1 SLA-satisfaction probabilities resemble those publicly advertised by that provider. Candidate models are compared with the published probabilities across the advertised regions, horizons and compliance thresholds using root mean squared error (RMSE). The search uses a Tree-structured Parzen Estimator (TPE) to propose candidate parameters. It has no access to the provider's internal traces or generating parameters. Consequently, its objective is to construct a model consistent with the disclosed evidence, not necessarily to recover the provider's true implementation.

In the expanded multi-world evaluation (historical \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-5 V3b}), M1 performs seven independent TPE searches per provider, each evaluating 25 candidate configurations using 15 local simulation runs per candidate. The seven selected candidates are then reevaluated using a fresh common bank of 100 provider-local simulations. The candidate with the lowest I1 RMSE is retained for each provider. A second independent local bank is used only for diagnostics, not selection. Finally, the three selected provider models are combined and simulated within the native composition graph to estimate the requested end-to-end SLA probability. The historical \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-3}/4 implementation used a different search procedure, summarized in the method-version matrix.

\subsection*{4.3 M2: equal-weight finite model propagation}

Like M1, M2 reconstructs stochastic provider models from public disclosures and simulates their composition using the known graph to estimate end-to-end SLA satisfaction. The difference is that M2 retains three reconstructed models per provider instead of one. It then simulates all $3^3=27$ combinations of the three providers' models and averages the resulting graph-level predictions with equal weights:

\begin{equation*}
\widehat\sigma_G^{M2}(A_G,H;\rho)
=\frac{1}{27}\sum_{j=1}^{27}
\widehat\sigma_G(A_G,H;\rho\mid\vartheta_j).
\tag{10}
\end{equation*}

Here $\vartheta_j$ denotes one joint model containing a reconstructed model for each provider, and the conditional prediction is obtained by simulating that model on graph $G$ for the requested region $A_G$, horizon $H$ and compliance threshold $\rho$. Equal weighting is a design choice, not an estimate of the probability that any reconstructed model is the true provider implementation.

In the expanded evaluation across multiple provider configurations and graphs (historical \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-5 V3b}), M2 uses the same seven I1-fitted candidates per provider described in Section 4.2 and retains the three with the lowest common-bank I1 RMSE. Each provider's M1-selected model is therefore included in M2's support, and the M1 joint model is one of the 27 combinations. This selection has no explicit diversity requirement or hard I1-compatibility threshold. The historical \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-4} M2 instead selected diverse candidates and excluded the M1 anchor, as recorded in the method-version matrix.

Each of the 27 joint models is simulated for 100 graph trajectories, giving \textbf{2,700 graph trajectories per physical cell}. Shared seed indices across member simulations must be respected in numerical uncertainty estimates (Section 9.1). By retaining several provider reconstructions selected for their agreement with the public I1 evidence, M2 reduces the dependence of the graph-level prediction on a single fitted model. The hypothesis is that averaging predictions from these alternative reconstructions can mitigate errors specific to one model selection, especially when the disclosed evidence does not clearly favor a unique reconstruction.

\textbf{Reconstruction with enriched provider information.} M2 depends on the quality of the reconstructed provider models. Even when several models are retained, I1 discloses only probabilities of satisfying a finite set of compliance thresholds. We therefore investigated whether richer public evidence could better constrain provider reconstruction and improve subsequent graph-level prediction. I2a discloses the distribution of compliance across runs at each horizon, while I2b additionally discloses paired compliance fractions at two horizons within the same run (Section 3.2).

Six reconstruction variants were evaluated using the same M2 aggregation rule in Eq. (10). All use the same provider-model family and parameter domains, retain three models per provider, and propagate their 27 joint combinations with equal weights. The I1STRONG\_M2 variant provides an I1-based reference under the same strengthened search budget. The variants differ in the \textbf{disclosed evidence used for fitting and the criterion used to select provider models}.

For each provider and variant, the matched search evaluates 256 parameter settings: 64 initial space-filling proposals followed by 192 adaptive proposals. Each candidate is scored with 25 provider-local simulation runs; the best 24 are rescored on a fresh common bank of 100 runs, and the best three are retained. The 27 resulting joint models are each simulated for 100 graph trajectories, giving \textbf{2,700 graph trajectories per variant and physical cell}. Search budgets and random-number banks are matched, although adaptive proposals can diverge because their fitting scores differ. \subsection*{4.4 M3: public-evidence-weighted finite reconstruction}

M1 selects one reconstructed model per provider, and M2 averages predictions from three equally weighted models per provider. M3 instead retains a larger set of candidate reconstructions and gives more influence to those that better reproduce the provider's public I1 information. The motivation is to retain alternative explanations of the disclosed evidence without assuming they should all contribute equally.

In the expanded evaluation, \textbf{M3 uses the same seven provider candidates generated by the reconstruction procedure in Section 4.2}. No additional provider-parameter search is performed for M3. Whereas M1 selects the lowest-I1-RMSE candidate and M2 retains the three lowest-I1-RMSE candidates, M3 retains all seven and computes a separate evidence-based weight for each. All use the same gamma-process family and parameterization in Eq. (9).

To measure agreement with I1, let $\mathcal R_i$ be the advertised region, compliance-threshold and horizon combinations with $H>0$ for provider $i$. At observation $r\in\mathcal R_i$, let $p_{i,r}$ be the published probability of satisfying the SLA requirement and $q_{i,k,r}$ the corresponding probability simulated from candidate $k$. M3 uses the mean Bernoulli Kullback--Leibler (KL) discrepancy

\begin{equation*}
\begin{aligned}
E_{i,k}
&=\frac{1}{|\mathcal R_i|}\sum_{r\in\mathcal R_i}
d_{\mathrm B}(\widetilde p_{i,r},\widetilde q_{i,k,r}),\\
d_{\mathrm B}(p,q)
&=p\log\frac{p}{q}+(1-p)\log\frac{1-p}{1-q}.
\end{aligned}
\tag{11}
\end{equation*}

The tildes indicate Jeffreys smoothing: for an empirical probability $p$ estimated from $N$ runs, $\widetilde p=(Np+1/2)/(N+1)$, and likewise for $q$ using its own number of runs. In the expanded evaluation both the published I1 estimates and the common-bank candidate estimates use $N=100$. Smoothing avoids zero or one probabilities producing undefined or infinite logarithmic discrepancies.

\textbf{Why KL rather than RMSE?} RMSE measures absolute discrepancies between advertised and reconstructed compliance probabilities, regardless of their values. However, the same absolute error can have different implications for reliability. For example, predicting 0.99 when the advertised compliance probability is 0.90 underestimates the probability of noncompliance by a factor of ten, whereas a comparable error near 0.50 has a much smaller relative effect. Bernoulli KL penalizes such discrepancies more strongly because it measures the excess expected logarithmic loss of a candidate's probability predictions. We use this property to give less weight to reconstructed models that make overconfident predictions inconsistent with the public evidence. Whether this weighting improves graph-level prediction compared with RMSE-based weighting remains an empirical question not isolated by the present experiments.

Candidate $k$ for provider $i$ receives the normalized weight

\begin{equation*}
w_{i,k}
=\frac{\exp[-\lambda E_{i,k}]}
{\sum_{k'}\exp[-\lambda E_{i,k'}]},
\tag{12}
\end{equation*}

where $\lambda\ge0$ determines the concentration of weights: $\lambda=0$ gives equal weights, while larger values favor smaller KL discrepancies. In the original pilot, $\lambda=30$ was selected by five-fold cross-validation on the public I1 probability surface, without access to the graph white-box reference. The expanded evaluation \textbf{reused the value 30 without repeating that cross-validation}; Section 8.2 reports the consequences of that protocol difference. These Gibbs weights express relative support within a finite candidate bank; they are \textbf{not calibrated posterior probabilities} that candidates are the hidden generating models.

For a joint reconstruction $\vartheta_j=(\theta_{A,a},\theta_{B,b},\theta_{C,c})$, M3 multiplies the three provider weights:

\begin{equation*}
W_{a,b,c}=w_{A,a}w_{B,b}w_{C,c}.
\tag{13}
\end{equation*}

This factorization concerns weights assigned to candidate provider models. It does not assume independent graph execution: queueing, request histories and branch dependencies remain coupled within the native simulation of each joint model. For a propagated support $S$, with normalized weights $\alpha_j=W_j/\sum_{k\in S}W_k$, M3 predicts

\begin{equation*}
\widehat\sigma_G^{M3}(A_G,H;\rho)
=\sum_{j\in S}\alpha_j
\widehat\sigma_G(A_G,H;\rho\mid\vartheta_j).
\tag{14}
\end{equation*}

The \textbf{initial controlled pilot (historical M3-v4)} used a different candidate-generation procedure: 48 Latin-hypercube-derived candidates per provider rather than seven independently fitted candidates. With public-I1 Gibbs weights at $\lambda=30$, it propagated the \textbf{Top14} joint models using graph budgets of $B=1400$ or $2000$. Those 14 members held about 99.917\% of the weight \textbf{within that finite inferred distribution}, not of the probability of the true provider system.

The subsequent expanded evaluation (historical \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-5 V3b}) used the seven candidates per provider described above and propagated \textbf{all $7^3=343$ joint models} with total graph budget $B=1400$. The initially registered Top14 approximation was replaced before the final graph white-box results were read because a permitted pre-graph diagnostic found that Top14 retained only 0.552 of the inferred mass in P1, 0.962 in P3 and 0.867 in P4. This preserved the candidate bank and Gibbs weights, but changed numerical propagation and therefore remains a \textbf{documented method revision}, not an unchanged test of the original Top14 method.

For any retained subset of total Gibbs weight $m_S$, omitting the remaining reconstructed models changes the exact weighted-portfolio prediction by at most $1-m_S$. The maximum difference was approximately 0.000826 for the original pilot Top14 and zero for FULL343 within its own 343-member bank; neither value bounds prediction error relative to the real composed service.

\textbf{Allocation of the graph-simulation budget.} After weighting the reconstructed joint models, M3 still needs graph simulations to estimate their individual SLA probabilities. In the expanded evaluation, all 343 joint models share a budget of $B=1400$ trajectories. Allocating the same number of runs to every model would ignore their unequal influence on the weighted prediction. Instead, M3 allocates runs to reduce an upper bound on the prediction's Monte Carlo variance.

For a propagated support $S$ with normalized weights $\alpha_j$ (Eq. (14)), let $N_j$ be the number of independent graph trajectories simulated for model $j$. Each trajectory records whether the requested compliance target is met. Assuming independent simulation estimates across joint models, the conditional numerical variance satisfies

\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{Var}_{MC}(\widehat\sigma_G^{M3})
\le\frac14\sum_{j\in S}\frac{\alpha_j^2}{N_j},
\qquad \sum_{j\in S}N_j=B.
\tag{15}
\end{equation*}

For fixed models, weights and simulation counts, the bound uses the elementary fact that a Bernoulli indicator with success probability $p_j$ has variance $p_j(1-p_j)\le1/4$. Minimizing this \textbf{worst-case numerical variance bound} with a continuous trajectory budget gives $N_j=B\alpha_j$, a special case of the weighted allocation principle of Neyman (1934; Appendix D). This is not an allocation optimized for the unknown member-specific variances.

For integer counts $N_j\ge1$, adding one trajectory to member $j$ reduces the bound by $\alpha_j^2/[4N_j(N_j+1)]$. M3 starts with one trajectory per joint model and assigns each remaining trajectory to the largest reduction, with deterministic rank-based ties. This is a marginal-priority integer allocation of the kind studied by Wright (2014; Appendix D). The factor $1/4$ is common to all members and can be omitted when ranking priorities. In the executed FULL343 support, $\alpha_j=W_j$, giving the implemented rule $\Delta_j=W_j^2/[N_j(N_j+1)]$.

A separate read-only verification script \path{verify_method_support_v0_1.py} reproduced the archived 343-member, $B=1400$ allocation counts for P1, P3 and P4; its findings are recorded in \path{METHOD_SUPPORT_VERIFICATION_REVIEW_2026-10-16.md} (Appendix B.1). This checks agreement between the saved allocations and the specified rule. It does \textbf{not} demonstrate improved prediction accuracy, verify every simulation trajectory or address error caused by the reconstructed provider models.

\textbf{M3 Top1 is not necessarily M1.} M1 selects by minimum I1 RMSE, while M3's highest-weight joint model is determined by the product of KL-based provider weights. More generally, comparing M3 with M2 also changes candidate coverage, weighting loss and graph-simulation allocation. An observed difference in prediction accuracy therefore does not isolate the effect of KL weighting alone.

\section*{5. Native simulator, graph structures, and experiment protocol}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{5. Native simulator, graph structures, and experiment protocol}

\subsection*{5.1 AICon-based simulation framework and composition extensions}

The experiments use AICon (Agentic Intelligence for the Computing Continuum) [11], built on a modified fork of YAFS (Yet Another Fog Simulator) [9]. YAFS provides a Python-based discrete-event environment for simulating applications across edge, fog and cloud infrastructure, including network communication, service execution and queueing. AICon adds a management-agent layer through which agents can observe simulated system conditions and intervene in their operation.

Why use AICon rather than extend YAFS directly? The choice connects this performance-prediction testbed to the broader research programme on Composable Autonomic Offerings (CAO) [12]. In future work, management agents could use runtime observations and performance predictions to investigate \textbf{local resilience}: detecting degradation and considering changes to service offerings or compositions before operational objectives are lost. This is a direction for subsequent CAO experiments, \textbf{not a capability validated by the present results}. In particular, the prediction accuracy demonstrated here is not assumed sufficient for dependable autonomous intervention.

For the present study, the modified AICon/YAFS runtime provides service-composition mechanisms beyond individual service execution. It registers branches and their execution dependencies, tracks the progress of each composition request separately, releases a dependent branch only after the required predecessor results for that request have arrived, and emits a single result when all required branches complete. Standalone engineering probes exercise joins, isolation between concurrent composition requests and nested composition behavior. These mechanisms let us measure end-to-end service outcomes under sequential and parallel dependencies, including the effects of queueing and network delays.

A second extension introduces \textbf{stochastic instruction demand} for simulated application modules. Instead of assigning the same instruction count to every invocation, a message can carry a distribution from which the instruction count is sampled when that message is instantiated. The simulator converts the sampled instruction demand into execution time using the processing capacity of the infrastructure node executing the application module, expressed in instructions per simulation time unit (IPT). In these experiments, provider work is Gamma-distributed, with a deterministic case when the specified variation is zero [23]. Thus, successive requests can take different times on the same infrastructure, with the resulting variation interacting with queueing and composition dependencies.

The expanded \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-5} evaluation (Phase II) tests the prediction methods beyond the initial single-world, single-graph pilot, across several service-composition graphs $G$ and provider worlds. Here $G$ denotes the \textbf{logical dependencies among provider services}. The battery considers four fixed three-provider graphs: fully parallel, fully sequential, and two mixed sequence/parallel structures. For each provider world, the four compositions use the same physical infrastructure and provider placements. Only the execution dependencies among the providers change. Periodic arrivals trigger stochastic provider services, with native queueing, service cost and network delays. The experiment covers these four structures, rather than arbitrary nested service graphs; Section 5.2 describes the provider-world and SLA-query variations.

\subsection*{5.2 Experimental design}
\phantomsection\label{sec:experimental-design}

To evaluate the prediction methods under different conditions, we considered four provider configurations (P1--P4) and the four service-composition graphs introduced in Section 5.1, giving 16 planned test cases. For Step-0, a \emph{query condition} $(A,\rho)$ fixes the admissibility region and required compliance fraction, but not the horizon; evaluating it at a particular $H$ gives a complete prediction query $(A,H;\rho)$ (\hyperref[sec:prediction-target]{Section~2.2}). Step-0 classified these conditions by their estimated probability of meeting the compliance requirement: higher probabilities meant easier conditions, and lower probabilities harder. For each condition, this probability was estimated using preliminary white-box simulations and averaged over the 37 horizons $H=60,65,\ldots,240$ seconds. For each of five fixed compliance thresholds $\rho$, it sought one Easy condition (mean probability in $[0.95,1.00]$), one Mid condition ($[0.75,0.90]$), and one Stress condition ($[0.40,0.75]$). Adjacent regime means had to differ by at least 0.10. Candidate conditions were obtained by scaling the latency and cost limits of the publicly composed baseline SLA region by $s=0.50,0.51,\ldots,2.00$, keeping the quality limit fixed, and requiring $s_{\mathrm{Stress}}<s_{\mathrm{Mid}}<s_{\mathrm{Easy}}$.

For each test case, a method-independent calibration bank of 200 white-box trajectories selected candidates closest to the respective regime target centers. A separate, disjoint bank of 200 trajectories then had to confirm the same probability bands and minimum separations for all 15 query conditions. If any required condition could not be selected or confirmed, the whole world-by-graph case was excluded without reselection or replacement. All four P2 cases failed Step-0, leaving 12 cases for evaluation. These Step-0 trajectories served query design only; they were not used to fit the prediction methods or as the final white-box reference.

For each retained case, we evaluated 15 query conditions at 37 observation horizons, from 60 to 240 seconds in steps of five seconds. This gives 555 probability estimates per case and 6,660 across the 12 cases. These measurements are related because different SLA conditions and horizons can be evaluated using the same simulation trajectories.

The prediction methods received the published provider information and the composition graph, but not the underlying provider models. Their predictions were recorded before being compared with a separate reference generated from 1,000 new simulations of each composition using its actual provider models. This reference, which we call \emph{white-box}, provides an independent estimate of the SLA satisfaction probability against which prediction accuracy can be measured.

\subsection*{5.3 Evaluation safeguards and reproducibility}

As summarized in \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Appendix C}, the research progressed from a controlled pilot (Phase I) to a broader evaluation (Phase II) and subsequent retrospective investigations (Phase III). Here we focus on how the expanded evaluation separated predictions from their reference results, what the recorded evidence verifies, and which reproducibility limitations remain.

To evaluate prediction accuracy, the predictions must be recorded before the reference results used to assess them become available. For each of the 12 eligible test cases, the methods first estimated end-to-end SLA satisfaction using the information published by the providers. These predictions were then fixed, before a separate set of simulations using the underlying provider models generated the reference probabilities. This separation allowed the predictions to be evaluated without using the final reference results to construct them.

A subsequent audit examined whether this separation was supported by the saved experimental records. For all 12 cases, the saved prediction files matched the hashes recorded when predictions were frozen, and the final-reference records linked back to that same frozen prediction set.

The methods and procedures also changed during development. Provider-model reconstruction and the integration of reconstructed models were revised in response to earlier diagnostics, before the final reference results were examined. Some engineering checks were completed after scientific experiments had already begun. Appendix C identifies these revisions and their scientific roles, so the expanded evaluation should not be interpreted as an unchanged test of the original plan. The repository retains source versions, simulation configurations and verification records, but the exact AICon/YAFS software state used in every historical run has not been fully established. Reference [23] identifies the audited AICon checkout; complete bitwise reproduction of the original runs is therefore not claimed.

\section*{6. Controlled pilot (Phase I)}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{6. Controlled pilot (Phase I)}

The controlled pilot (Phase I) provided the first evaluation of the prediction methods using a single provider configuration and composition graph. It established an initial comparison of their prediction accuracy under fixed experimental conditions. The results also motivated the broader evaluation presented in the following section. As detailed in Appendix C, the pilot and subsequent evaluations involved different method versions and therefore serve distinct scientific purposes.

\subsection*{6.1 Construction of the pilot reference}

The pilot evaluated one fixed service composed of three providers operating in parallel, with a new request generated every 0.2 seconds. To measure prediction accuracy, we needed reference probabilities for the actual composed service. We therefore simulated it repeatedly using the underlying provider models, which were not available to the prediction methods. Each simulation produced request outcomes from which we could assess end-to-end SLA compliance at different observation horizons. The provider models and their published I1 information remained fixed throughout the pilot.

The simulation outcomes could be assessed against many different SLA requirements, so we first had to choose which requirements to test. In the original pilot, we used the advertised provider limits as a starting point. We combined them using M0's aggregation rules (Section 4.1): for this parallel graph, the baseline latency is the largest provider latency plus fixed delays, the costs are added together with fixed costs, and the baseline quality is the lowest provider quality. This defines the baseline SLA region $A_G^{\rm base}(\rho)=A_G^{I1+M0}(\rho)$. We then scaled its latency and cost limits by a factor $\gamma$, leaving the quality limit unchanged:

\begin{equation*}
A_G(\gamma,\rho)
=\{L_G\le\gamma\ell_G^{\rm base}(\rho),\
C_G\le\gamma c_G^{\rm base}(\rho),\
Q_G\ge q_G^{\rm base}(\rho)\}.
\tag{16}
\end{equation*}

The scaling factor specifies the difficulty of the requested SLA: $\gamma>1$ allows more latency and cost, whereas $\gamma<1$ imposes tighter limits. We combined three such difficulty levels with five compliance thresholds $\rho$, giving 15 SLA conditions. Each condition was evaluated at 37 observation horizons, from 60 to 240 seconds, using the same bank of $N_{\rm WB}=200$ independent simulation trajectories. For each trajectory and horizon, we calculated the fraction of decided requests satisfying the requested SLA region. The reference probability was the proportion of trajectories in which that fraction reached the required threshold $\rho$. Because the same trajectories were evaluated repeatedly, these reference measurements are related.

Using M0's aggregation rules to choose the SLA test conditions is separate from using M0 to predict SLA satisfaction. The reference probabilities were obtained from simulations of the actual composed service, independently of M0's predictions.

\section*{7. Expanded evaluation (Phase II)}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{7. Expanded evaluation (Phase II)}

\subsection*{7.1 Eligibility and exact prediction population}

\hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-5} planned 16 physical world-by-graph cells. As described in \hyperref[sec:experimental-design]{Section~5.2}, Step-0 used preliminary simulations of the underlying provider models to select informative SLA conditions and confirm each cell's eligibility. \subsection*{7.3 Topology and provider-world effects}

The prospectively specified topology statistic for each method and provider world was

\begin{equation*}
\Delta_P^M =
\operatorname{MAE}_M(P,G_{\mathrm{SEQ}})
-
\operatorname{MAE}_M(P,G_{\mathrm{PAR}}).
\tag{17}
\end{equation*}

The original directional masking hypothesis anticipated $\Delta_P^M>0$. The completed analysis did \textbf{not} yield that sign consistently across the eligible worlds: P1 differed in sign from P3/P4 for the examined contrast. It is therefore not supported as a universal or robust rule by this evaluation. A second registered heterogeneous-minus-symmetric contrast required P2 and was \textbf{not estimable} after Step-0 exclusion. The two other graph ASTs offer mechanistic triangulation but do not constitute a clean factorial manipulation separating workload shift from aggregation structure.

The 6,660 measurements reuse simulation trajectories across SLA conditions and time horizons, so they are not independent experiments. They cannot be treated as 6,660 independent physical deployments when assessing variation across provider configurations. A WB-reference bootstrap resamples the \textbf{complete trajectory/seed index} jointly across horizons, queries and shared-seed cells, keeping method predictions fixed. It addresses finite-$N_{\mathrm{WB}}$ reference fluctuation, \textbf{not model-selection uncertainty or future-world robustness}.

The scientifically material result is twofold: a weighted finite ensemble maintained a favorable aggregate \emph{ranking} among the executed methods, and its \textbf{absolute prediction remained too inaccurate in several difficult regions} to claim dependable generic SLA estimation.

\section*{9. Uncertainty mathematics and cross-experiment interpretation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{9. Uncertainty mathematics and cross-experiment interpretation}

\subsection*{9.1 Numerical Monte Carlo precision}

For a fixed reconstructed ensemble with normalized weights $\alpha_j$ and estimates $\widehat p_j$, let $\widehat p_M=\sum_j\alpha_j\widehat p_j$. The variance identity is

\begin{equation*}
V_{\rm MC}(\widehat p_M)
=\sum_j\alpha_j^2 V_j+
2\sum_{j<k}\alpha_j\alpha_k C_{jk}.
\tag{18}
\end{equation*}

The covariance $C_{jk}$ matters for \textbf{M2's shared seed indices}. A seed-index paired bootstrap should resample each 27-member seed-vector as one unit rather than resampling individual member trajectories independently. M3's independent member seed streams permit the simpler variance form in Eq. (15), conditional on fixed weights, models and allocation.

The pilot's numerical-width appendix used Wilson intervals for fixed single-model binomial curves, paired percentile resampling for M2 and a normal approximation for M3 using a \textbf{worst-case variance}. The variance bound itself is conservative; the resulting \textbf{normal-approximation 95\% interval is not an exact distribution-free confidence interval}, particularly when the FULL343 allocation leaves some models with only one trajectory. All such intervals are \textbf{pointwise} over $(A,H,\rho)$ and conditional on the reconstructed method, not simultaneous across all 555 or 6,660 related SLA measurements.

Separately, the Wilson intervals included in I1 measure sampling uncertainty in the provider-side probability estimates conditional on regions fitted from the independent construction bank. They do not include uncertainty in the fitted regions or provide simultaneous coverage across the I1 probability surface.

A rigorous fixed-sample conditional alternative, if required in later work, can be derived from a weighted Hoeffding inequality for independent bounded member outcomes, but this was \textbf{not an evaluated new estimator} in the frozen experiments. Sequentially peeking at ordinary fixed-sample intervals until a threshold is crossed does not preserve nominal coverage without a separately specified anytime-valid procedure.

\subsection*{9.2 Model dispersion is a distinct diagnostic}

Let $p_j=E[\widehat p_j]$, $p_\alpha=\sum_j\alpha_jp_j$ and $\widehat p=\sum_j\alpha_j\widehat p_j$. The observed finite-ensemble dispersion is

\begin{equation*}
V_{\rm raw}
=\sum_j\alpha_j(\widehat p_j-\widehat p)^2.
\tag{19}
\end{equation*}

Under fixed weights and unbiased member estimators, its expectation equals latent finite-model spread plus a contribution from numerical sampling,

\begin{equation*}
E[V_{\rm raw}]
=\sum_j\alpha_j(p_j-p_\alpha)^2+
\sum_j\alpha_j\operatorname{Var}(\widehat p_j)
-\operatorname{Var}(\widehat p).
\tag{20}
\end{equation*}

The manuscript's nonnegative MC-subtracted plug-in diagnostic estimates only the \emph{spread among the selected models}. The max-with-zero clipping and finite member budgets make it neither an unbiased calibrated risk estimate nor a guaranteed bound on model-to-hidden-world error.

Similarly, if the method predictor and white-box reference are independent and individually unbiased for their conditional targets, then

\begin{equation*}
E\!\left[(\widehat p_M-\widehat p_{\rm WB})^2\right]
=(p_M-\sigma_G)^2+V_{\rm MC,M}+V_{\rm WB}.
\tag{21}
\end{equation*}

Subtracting empirical noise estimates from an observed squared difference and clipping at zero is an \textbf{experimental noise-correction diagnostic}. It does not make the unobserved model approximation error known exactly, and its square root adds estimation bias.

\subsection*{9.3 Cross-phase lessons}

These lessons compare global \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase I} (controlled pilot), \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase II} (expanded battery) and \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase III} (retrospective divergence tracing) while preserving their distinct reference populations and evidence status.

\textbf{Candidate search is a plausible limiting mechanism, demonstrably so in P2.} The fact that true parameters better fit I1 than selected candidates makes the inverse search procedure a high-priority diagnosis. It does not eliminate other causes of graph discrepancy, including information compression and model-family restrictions.

\textbf{Ensembling is neither futile nor proved necessary.} The pilot same-support weighting test favored nonuniform public-evidence weights over uniform weights, while pilot Top1 slightly outperformed the larger weighted mixture at a matched budget. In the larger \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-5} study M3 FULL343 had the best aggregate MAE but still made sizeable errors. The data support context-dependent method performance, not a model-count law.

\textbf{Internal mixture coverage is not external prediction validity.} The retrospective original-style Top14 captured over 99.6\% of the inferred joint-model mass in each eligible provider world, yet its overall MAE exceeded V3b FULL343. This does not isolate member count because the candidate-fitting and weighting pipelines changed.

\textbf{Richer disclosure is not sufficient by itself.} The I2 development variants were matched in broad search effort but changed loss functions and used an already studied cell. Their results test implemented pipelines, not the optimal value of their disclosed statistics.

\textbf{Graph structure matters without a simple universal directional rule.} A registered serial-versus-parallel masking prediction did not retain one direction across eligible worlds. The finite design does not identify a universal composition-graph error mechanism.

\textbf{Numerical convergence is insufficient for dependability.} Larger graph budget can reduce finite simulation noise without changing bias due to model/search error. No threshold band yet covers the total uncertainty required for a certified real-service admission decision.

\section*{10. Threats to validity and reproducibility}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{10. Threats to validity and reproducibility}

\textbf{Finite physical setting.} The core study uses three providers and four logical ASTs on a fixed physical embedding. It does not generalize by itself to arbitrary service graphs, cross-region placement, multi-tenant failures, dynamic routing or real production deployments. The provider generators and fitted process family are related; robustness to substantial model misspecification is not established.

\textbf{Limited observables.} I1 is deliberately compressed and empirical. Region construction was calibrated in frozen simulated worlds; its disclosure noise and dependence on the acquisition policy can affect inverse fits. Quality and power are part of the broader conceptual LCQ/continuum setting but not demonstrated as independent varying stress dimensions here.

\textbf{I1 acquisition verification.} The I1 acquisition procedure was checked against its frozen implementation and provenance records, but the complete acquisition was not independently reproduced from raw simulation events. Moreover, the actual coverage of the constructed regions in the generating provider worlds was not separately validated against the target probability content of the fitted latency-cost models.

\textbf{Condition selection and missing P2.} The \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-5} registered Step-0 eligibility gate failed for P2 and removed four of 16 intended physical cells. Main 12-cell estimates are \textbf{conditional on eligibility}, not estimates of all planned or unseen worlds. The P2-derived retrospective tests use new query regions and are not missing-data imputation.

\textbf{Method changes and development leakage.} V3, V3b and FULL343 changed model search, selection and support after earlier development evidence. The final graph WB was held back behind a hashed global prediction freeze, and archived timestamps/hash links were checked, but this does not restore the stronger status of having all methods fixed before every component of experimental evidence was observed. The same cell was reused while developing \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-6} I2 variants.

\textbf{Retrospective original-style M3.} The B1400 Top14 and Top1 comparison was specified after prior V3b outcomes were known and reused the same reference. Different provider proposal/fit and local costs prevent causal attribution to ensemble size. The archived audit omits raw native graph ledgers, so it verifies included results rather than bitwise replay.

\textbf{Budget and correlation.} M1/M2/M3 graph budgets differ (100/2700/1400); their numbers of locally simulated trajectories and method fitting time differ as well. The 555 and 6,660 SLA measurements reuse simulation trajectories and query definitions, so they are not independent. Reference bootstrap and per-method numerical error do not simultaneously quantify scenario selection, model-search and physical-world extrapolation uncertainty.

\textbf{Engineering and software provenance.} The modified composition controller is in an external AICon/YAFS repository. The local current dependency revision and source SHA fingerprints were recorded, but exact clean-tree/source-lock provenance at each archived October run was not recovered. Several required smoke checks were completed only after early scientific work, a sequencing deviation documented in the audit. The \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-5} graph simulator received optional trace-exposure updates after main runs, so current repository HEAD alone does not identify the historical program state.

\textbf{Numerical evidence tier.} Primary saved result summaries and selected hashes have been independently checked against existing pointwise artifacts. This is not complete bitwise replay from every raw YAFS request event. Some ancillary historical pilot operating-point curves and an M2 paired-bootstrap width remain as reported source claims rather than independently recomputed numerical evidence. No statement above requires inventing their raw observations.

\textbf{External literature and coauthor consent.} The bibliography is inherited from the source manuscript and is \textbf{provisional}. References, citation details, current related work and venue overlap policy must be verified before any public release. This draft is internal and does not constitute coauthor approval for arXiv posting.

\section*{11. Conclusions and falsifiable next questions}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{11. Conclusions and falsifiable next questions}

Across \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase I} (controlled pilot), \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase II} (expanded battery) and \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase III} (retrospective divergence tracing), the PRAISE program implemented a discrete-event native composition benchmark, finite public provider SLA disclosures and multiple reconstructed-model integration procedures. It established robust provenance for important frozen outputs, including a hash-frozen prediction/reference separation in the broad \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase-5} battery. The favorable single-world pilot numerical findings were genuine, but the broader evaluation showed \textbf{material residual prediction errors} and did not establish general reconstruction sufficiency.

The retrospective M3 comparison shows that near-complete model-bank mass coverage cannot ensure external SLA accuracy. \hyperref[app:experiment-stages]{Phase III} then established bounded pilot estimator/native-graph parity and documented altered candidate domains and transferred rather than reselected $\lambda=30$. These are verified protocol differences, not a unique causal explanation. A new holdout with controlled reconstruction ablations is needed to distinguish the causes.

The most actionable scientific questions now concern mechanisms rather than adding model members indiscriminately:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Search recoverability:} When true generating parameters lie inside the search domain and fit I1 markedly better, which proposal distributions, loss landscapes or structural changes allow a public-only learner to find equivalent-quality fits with modest budget? This must be tested with a frozen search protocol and separate end-to-end graph reference.
\item \textbf{Information conditional value:} Can a fixed reconstruction objective/architecture be defined so that I1 and I2 disclosures differ \emph{only} in the information made available, permitting an interpretable matched-information comparison?
\item \textbf{Graph sensitivity:} Which structural or workload conditions amplify small provider-level errors into end-to-end SLA error, and can the resulting condition be predicted using \textbf{publicly observable} quantities rather than privileged white-box outcomes?
\item \textbf{Finite-model approximation:} Under what observable diagnostics does Top1 suffice, when do additional hypotheses help and when does weighting mislead? Any rule proposed from a development world must be frozen and tested on other worlds.
\item \textbf{Decision calibration:} Can a method produce externally validated error coverage for the hidden graph SLA under clearly limited assumptions? This is a separate problem from making the Monte Carlo standard error small.
\end{enumerate}

These questions are \textbf{future hypotheses} about the specific SLA-prediction task, not positive claims established by the existing evaluation. More broadly, the pilot-to-battery differences, excluded Step-0 cases, unequal method budgets, dependent measurements and multiple uncertainty sources provide \textbf{early empirical evidence motivating a separate methodological study}. That future paper is intended to develop and validate a systematic process for choosing representative and challenging hierarchical graphs and operating conditions, determining simulation and reference sample sizes, and measuring estimation errors and uncertainties when evaluating management, control and optimization algorithms in the computing continuum. None of those design rules is established by the present report. Publication of this evidence record as a citable preprint remains a subsequent coauthor and venue-policy decision.

\section*{Appendix A. Mathematical and numerical interpretation}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Appendix A. Mathematical and numerical interpretation}

\subsection*{A.1 Finite-portfolio admission envelopes}

\noindent\fcolorbox{green!50!black}{green!23}{%
\parbox{\dimexpr\linewidth-2\fboxsep-2\fboxrule\relax}{%
\textbf{\textcolor{green!30!black}{REVISIT APPENDIX A.1 WHEN REVIEWING THIS SECTION.}}\\
The historical $\beta$-based admission-envelope interpretation below is retained provisionally. Decide whether to remove it or describe the finite-model extrema as a range of SLA-satisfaction predictions. Do not treat $\beta$ as part of the contracted SLA.%
}%
}

For a specified, finite method-generated set $\mathcal M_M$ of joint stochastic models, define

\begin{equation*}
\sigma^{-}_{G,M}(A,H;\rho)=\min_{\vartheta\in\mathcal M_M}
\sigma_G(A,H;\rho\mid\vartheta),\qquad
\sigma^{+}_{G,M}(A,H;\rho)=\max_{\vartheta\in\mathcal M_M}
\sigma_G(A,H;\rho\mid\vartheta).
\tag{A1}
\end{equation*}

If $\beta\le\sigma^-_{G,M}$ then \emph{all enumerated models} accept under their own ideal probabilities; if $\beta>\sigma^+_{G,M}$ all reject; otherwise there is disagreement among the finite set. This is a direct statement about the chosen set, \textbf{not} a calibrated confidence set, structural-identifiability theorem or SLA truth guarantee. Estimates of the extrema from finite graph simulations add MC error that is absent from this idealized algebra.

\subsection*{A.2 Conditional numerical precision and model spread}

The general covariance identity (18) holds for fixed finite weights. M2's paired common-seed bootstrap captures cross-model covariance; M3's independent-member variance bound (15) is conditional on the model bank, fixed weights and allocation. For member success probabilities $p_j$, independence across members and trajectories gives the exact conditional identity

\begin{equation*}
\mathrm{Var}_{MC}(\widehat\sigma_G^{M3})
=\sum_{j\in S}\frac{\alpha_j^2p_j(1-p_j)}{N_j}
\le\frac14\sum_{j\in S}\frac{\alpha_j^2}{N_j}.
\end{equation*}

For a positive normalized weight vector, Cauchy--Schwarz gives $\sum_j\alpha_j^2/N_j\ge(\sum_j\alpha_j)^2/B=1/B$, with equality at $N_j=B\alpha_j$ in the continuous relaxation. For integer allocations, the bound decrease from one extra run is $\alpha_j^2/[4N_j(N_j+1)]$, a diminishing quantity as $N_j$ grows. Greedily taking the largest available decrease therefore minimizes this separable bound among positive integer allocations at a fixed total $B$ (cf. Neyman, 1934; Wright, 2014, Appendix D). It need not minimize variance based on unknown $p_j(1-p_j)$ or physical-model error. For a fixed sample allocation and truly independent bounded member outcomes, a weighted Hoeffding inequality gives

\begin{equation*}
\Pr\bigl(|\widehat p-p_\alpha|\ge\varepsilon\bigr)
\le 2\exp\left(-\frac{2\varepsilon^2}{\sum_j\alpha_j^2/N_j}\right),
\tag{A2}
\end{equation*}

yielding a fixed-sample half-width

\begin{equation*}
\varepsilon_\delta=
\sqrt{\frac12\ln(2/\delta)\sum_j\frac{\alpha_j^2}{N_j}}.
\tag{A3}
\end{equation*}

This is included as an \emph{analytical consequence of standard bounded-variable concentration}, \textbf{not} a newly tested method or a bound on $|p_\alpha-\sigma_G|$. It is not automatically anytime-valid if sample sizes are chosen by repeatedly observing the outcomes.

The historical noise-adjusted model-spread quantities and white-box correction (19)–(21) are expectation-based diagnostics. Positive-part corrections may be convenient but must not be described as exact interval widths for physical model misspecification.

\subsection*{A.3 Numerical-width scaling}

The historical precision appendix extrapolated mean \textbf{pointwise interval width} approximately as $\overline W(B)=c/\sqrt B$, holding support, allocation and the simulation experiment fixed, so that a target width $w^\star$ corresponds formally to $B^\star\approx(c/w^\star)^2$. The pilot anchors were approximately M1 B100/width 0.0375; M2 B2700/width 0.0563 (not independently reproduced here from per-seed paired bootstrap arrays); M3 Top14 B1400/width 0.0493 and B2000/width 0.0425, using \textbf{interval clipping to [0,1]} for the reported M3 widths. These are illustrative numerical precision projections, \textbf{not evaluated accuracy-versus-budget forecasts}, and they exclude provider reconstruction expense and persistent model bias.
